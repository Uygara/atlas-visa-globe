How the Schengen 90/180-day rule really works

By Uygar Atalay · Updated 17 June 2026 · about a 7-minute read

If you visit Europe visa-free, the most important number in your trip is 90. You may spend up to 90 days in any 180-day period across the Schengen Area — and the way those 180 days are counted is where most people get it wrong. This guide explains the rolling window, walks through real examples, and shows how to stay on the right side of it.

What the rule actually says

The Schengen Area is a group of European countries that share a single external border and no internal passport checks. For most visa-free nationalities — and for holders of a short-stay Schengen visa — the allowance is the same: no more than 90 days of presence within any rolling 180-day window. It applies to the Area as a whole, not to each country. Three weeks in France, two in Italy and one in Spain all draw from the same 90-day budget.

The key word is "rolling". The 180 days are not a fixed calendar block that resets on a date. On any given day, you look backwards 180 days and add up how many of those you were inside the Area. That total must never exceed 90.

Why "90 days in, 90 days out" is a myth

A popular shortcut says you can stay 90 days, leave for 90 days, then return for another 90. Sometimes that works — but not always, because the window keeps moving. The only reliable method is to check, for the specific day you want to enter, whether you have spent fewer than 90 days inside the Area in the preceding 180.

Example 1 — a clean trip

You arrive on 1 March and leave on 29 May. That is 90 days exactly (the day you enter and the day you leave both count). You have used your full allowance. To enter again on any future date, at least some of those days must have "aged out" of the 180-day window so that the look-back total drops below 90.

Example 2 — splitting the year

You spend 60 days in spring, leave, and want to return in autumn. By the time you come back, those spring days may be more than 180 days in the past — in which case they no longer count and you have a fresh 90 available. If only some have aged out, you only get the difference. Always count from the planned entry date backwards.

Example 3 — the overstay trap

You stayed 85 days in the last few months and assume a quick 10-day trip is fine. But if 30 of those earlier days still fall inside the look-back window, you only have 60 left — and a 10-day visit is fine, but a 70-day one is an overstay. Border systems calculate this automatically; "I didn't realise" is not a defence.

Which countries count

The 90/180 budget covers the Schengen Area specifically — most of the EU plus Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Switzerland. It is not the same as the EU: Ireland runs its own separate visa policy and its days do not count toward Schengen. Microstates such as Monaco, San Marino and the Vatican have open borders with the Area and are treated as inside it. Bulgaria, Romania and Cyprus have been moving in and out of full membership in recent years, so check the current position for those.

What counts as a "day"

  • Both your entry day and exit day count as full days, even if you only spent an hour in the Area.
  • Time on a Schengen long-stay national visa or residence permit does not count against the 90/180 short-stay budget — those are a separate legal basis for being there.
  • A passport stamp is the primary evidence, so make sure you are stamped in and out.

How overstaying is penalised

Penalties vary by country but can include fines, a stamp that complicates future entries, and in serious cases an entry ban of one to several years recorded across the whole Area. From 2026 the EU's new Entry/Exit System records crossings biometrically, which makes accidental overstays far easier for authorities to spot. The safe approach is to build in a buffer and never plan a trip that uses the full 90 days unless you are certain of your history.

Frequently asked questions

Does a visa-free trip and a Schengen visa share the same 90 days?

Yes. Whether you enter visa-free or on a short-stay (type C) visa, the same 90-in-180 ceiling applies.

Can I reset the clock by hopping to a non-Schengen country?

Leaving the Area stops you accumulating days, but it does not erase the days you already spent — they still sit in the look-back window until they are more than 180 days old.

Do I need ETIAS as well?

From 2026 many visa-free travellers will also need an ETIAS authorization to enter — it is separate from, and additional to, the 90/180 limit.
