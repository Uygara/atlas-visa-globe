    Transit visas: when changing planes needs paperwork

    By Uygar Atalay · Updated 17 June 2026 · about a 6-minute read

    You are not even visiting the country — just changing planes for two hours. Surely that needs nothing? For most connections, that is true. But a number of countries require a transit visa even if you never leave the airport, and being caught out means being denied boarding at your departure city. Here is how to tell whether your layover is one of them.

    Airside vs landside: the crucial distinction

    There are two kinds of transit, and they have completely different rules.

    • Airside (international) transit — you stay inside the secure international zone, never pass immigration, and connect straight to your next flight. Many countries allow this freely.
    • Landside transit — you must clear immigration and re-check, for example because you change airports, collect and re-check baggage, or your bags are not through-tagged. The moment you cross the immigration line you are legally "entering", so you need whatever entry permission that country requires.
    First question to ask: will my bags be checked through to the final destination, and do I stay in the international zone? If yes, you are airside. If you must collect bags, change airports, or leave the secure area, you are landside — and the destination's normal entry rules apply.

    The airports that catch people out

    Even for pure airside transit, some countries require a specific transit visa from certain nationalities:

    • The Schengen Area operates an Airport Transit Visa (ATV) for a defined list of nationalities, even when connecting without entering.
    • The United Kingdom has a Direct Airside Transit Visa (DATV) requirement for many nationalities, and the list is not obvious — travellers regularly assume they are exempt when they are not.
    • The United States is the big one: it has no airside transit. There is no sterile international zone, so every traveller connecting through a US airport must clear immigration and therefore needs either visa-free entry (with ESTA) or a C-1 transit visa. A connection through the US is, in visa terms, an entry.
    • Canada requires an eTA or a transit visa for connections, depending on nationality and the transit programme used.

    When transit is time-limited

    Some countries allow short visa-free transit — for example a fixed number of hours to leave the airport and see the city, or visa-free airside transit only if your onward flight is within a set window. These "transit without visa" schemes are useful but specific: they often require a confirmed onward ticket within the time limit and may exclude certain nationalities. Treat the time limit and the conditions as strict.

    How to check your own connection

    1. Identify every country your itinerary touches, not just the start and end.
    2. For each, decide whether you will be airside or landside (bags through-checked? same airport? staying in the international zone?).
    3. Check the transit rule for your nationality at that airport — transit rules are nationality-specific.
    4. If in doubt, ask the airline: they are the ones who will deny boarding if you lack the right document, so their answer is the one that matters at the gate.

    Frequently asked questions

    Do I need a visa if I never leave the airport?

    Sometimes. Most countries allow free airside transit, but the Schengen Area, the UK and others require a transit visa from specific nationalities even airside — and the US has no airside transit at all.

    Does a layover in the US really need a visa or ESTA?

    Yes. Because there is no international transit zone, connecting through a US airport counts as entering the US, so you need visa-free entry with an approved ESTA, or a transit/visitor visa.

    What if I change airports in the same city?

    Changing airports almost always makes you landside — you leave the secure area and must satisfy the country's normal entry rules.
