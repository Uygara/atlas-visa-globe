    The six-month passport-validity rule, explained

    By Uygar Atalay · Updated 17 June 2026 · about a 5-minute read

    Your passport is valid — it does not expire until next month — and yet the airline refuses to let you board. This is the rule that surprises people most: many countries require your passport to be valid for a set period beyond the dates of your trip, most commonly six months. Here is how it works and how to make sure it never costs you a flight.

    What the rule says

    A large group of countries will not admit you unless your passport remains valid for at least six months after the day you arrive (some count from your departure date). The logic is that they do not want a visitor's passport expiring while they are in the country. It does not matter that your passport is technically still valid — if it has fewer than the required months left, you can be refused entry, or denied boarding by the airline acting on the country's behalf.

    The trap: a passport with three or four months left is "valid" for everyday purposes but fails the six-month rule. Many travellers only discover this at the airport, when it is too late to renew.

    How the period is counted

    There are two common ways countries count the buffer:

    • From the date of entry — your passport must be valid for six months starting the day you arrive. This is the most common version.
    • From the date of departure — the six months are measured from the day you intend to leave, which is slightly more generous.

    Because you cannot always tell in advance which method a border officer will apply, the safe assumption is the stricter one: count six months from your arrival date and make sure your passport covers it.

    Not every country uses six months

    • Six months is the most widespread requirement, common across much of Asia, the Middle East and parts of Africa.
    • Three months is used by many — the Schengen Area, for instance, requires validity of at least three months beyond your intended departure.
    • Valid for the stay — some countries only ask that your passport is valid for the duration of your visit, with no extra buffer.

    Because the requirement varies by destination, the only reliable approach is to check each country on your itinerary rather than assume one number covers them all.

    Other passport-condition pitfalls

    • Blank pages. Several countries require one or two completely blank visa pages on entry. A full passport can be refused even if it is in date.
    • Damage. A torn, water-damaged or unreadable passport may be rejected regardless of its expiry.
    • Renewal timing. When you renew, the new passport's number changes — update any eVisa, ESTA or ETIAS that was linked to the old one, or it will not match at the border.

    Practical advice

    The simplest habit is to renew your passport once it drops below nine months of validity if you travel at all regularly. That keeps you comfortably clear of every six-month rule and the renewal queue, and avoids the linked-visa problem of renewing right before a trip. Set a reminder for the nine-month mark rather than the expiry date itself.

    Frequently asked questions

    My passport is still valid — why was I refused?

    Because "valid" and "valid for six more months" are different tests. If your remaining validity is below the destination's required buffer, you fail the entry rule even though the passport has not expired.

    Does the Schengen Area need six months?

    No — the Schengen requirement is at least three months of validity beyond your planned departure (plus a passport issued within the previous ten years).

    I renewed my passport — do I need to redo my ESTA or eVisa?

    Yes, if the authorization was tied to the old passport number. Update or reapply so the document matches the passport you actually travel on.
