    How to read a passport's visa-free number

    By Uygar Atalay · Updated 24 September 2026 · about a 5-minute read

    Every passport page, and the side panel of the map, leads with one number. It looks simple, but a figure like "87 destinations" hides choices: does an eVisa count? A visa on arrival? A pre-travel registration such as ESTA? This guide spells out exactly what we count, how passports are ranked, and how to use the number without being misled.

    The headline number: no visa arranged in advance

    The big number is the count of destinations you can enter without arranging a visa in advance. It adds four of the seven entry categories on the map key:

    • Visa-free: you arrive with your passport and are admitted for a limited stay.
    • ID-card travel: some regional agreements let citizens cross with a national ID card instead of a passport.
    • Travel authorization: a quick online registration linked to your passport, such as the US ESTA or the Canadian eTA. It is a step you complete before flying, but it is not a visa application. We paint it in its own colour so it is never mistaken for plain visa-free travel.
    • Visa on arrival: you are issued the visa at the border.

    Why eVisas are counted separately

    An eVisa is a real visa that you apply for online. You need the approval before you travel, and processing can take days, so it sits outside the headline number. It is still far easier than a visit to an embassy, which is why every page shows a second, smaller line ("counting eVisas") that adds them. Destinations that need a visa from an embassy or consulate, and those that refuse entry altogether, are in neither number.

    One rule of thumb: the headline number is "places I can decide to go to today"; the number with eVisas is "places I can get to after a short online application".

    How passports are ranked

    The ranking sorts passports by the headline number. When two passports tie, the one with more destinations once eVisas are added ranks higher, and passports that tie on both share a position (1, 1, 3 and so on). The rank is always computed for the ordinary passport with no residence permits, even if you have switched on a permit or chosen a diplomatic passport in the panel.

    What your own map can change

    The side panel lets you add residence permits, combine several passports, or view a diplomatic or official passport. Those change your map and your tallies, because they change what you can really do, but not the ranking, which compares like with like. Passport types come from the "Non-ordinary passports" sections of the same public sources and only list destinations those sources name explicitly.

    Where the data comes from

    Visa requirements are read every day from public sources (the visa-requirement tables kept for each nationality) and then adjusted by curated rules for cases a table cannot express: ID-card travel between neighbouring countries, the travel-authorization label for the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and the United Kingdom, and a short list of sourced refusals. We never invent rules, fees or stay lengths; if a fact cannot be sourced we leave it out.

    Why our number can differ from other rankings

    Different rankings count differently. Some treat eVisas as access, some count travel authorizations as visa-free and others as visas, some include territories, and some update only a few times a year. So two different figures for the same passport can both be correct. Before you compare, check what each list counts.

    What the number does not tell you

    • How long you can stay. Visa-free stays run from a few weeks to several months and differ by destination. Open the country card.
    • That you will be admitted. Border officers decide, and entry conditions such as passport validity still apply.
    • What it costs or how long it takes. Where we hold fee and processing data, the country card shows it; where we do not, we say nothing rather than guess.

    Frequently asked questions

    Why does another site show a different number for my passport?

    Most likely it counts something differently. See the section above. Compare what each list includes before drawing a conclusion.

    Does the number change if I add a residence permit?

    Your map and tallies change, because the permit changes where you can go. The passport's rank does not.

    How often is it updated?

    Every day. Each passport page shows the date of the data it uses.
